> **The short answer:** feeling guilty every time you spend, even when you have enough, is usually not about money. It's about fear, and about never having decided what "enough" means for you. Give your savings a clear job, decide your number, then give yourself a planned amount to spend without having to justify it.

A couple in their early 40s wrote to me recently.

On paper, they're doing better than most people twice their age. They own their home, on a very low-interest loan. They have two apartments, fully paid off, that bring in rent every month. They pay into a pension. And they have €400,000 in savings.

Their words? "We still feel paralysed."

I see a version of this all the time, and not only with big numbers. People who earn well, who've done everything "right", and who still feel a knot in their stomach when they spend on anything that isn't strictly necessary.

If that sounds like you, this article is for you.

## Does this sound familiar?

- You compare prices on small things, even when the difference is a few cents.
- You buy something nice, then feel you have to explain it, or you return it.
- Your savings keep growing, but you never feel safer.
- Your partner or family has started to see your carefulness as sad rather than sensible.
- You keep postponing a holiday, a course or a treat "until things settle".

Being careful with money is a strength. But when saving stops being a choice and becomes a rule you can't break, something else is in charge.

## Firstly, it isn't really about money

In my experience, the guilt rarely comes from the bank balance. It usually comes from somewhere older.

Maybe you grew up in a home where money was tight and every purchase caused an argument. Maybe you saw a parent lose savings, a business or a job. Maybe you were taught, without anyone ever saying it, that spending on yourself is selfish.

Those lessons protected you once. But they don't know that your situation has changed. So they keep sounding the alarm, whatever the numbers say.

That's why more saving doesn't fix it. If the fear isn't about the amount, no amount will ever feel like enough.

## Decide what "enough" means to you

When I replied to that couple, I didn't suggest a single investment. I asked them two questions:

1. **What do you actually want out of life?**
2. **How much is enough?**

Most people have never answered these out loud. Without them, your savings have no finish line. And a race without a finish line is exhausting.

"Enough" isn't the same for everyone. For me, it means providing for my wife and children, seeing them grow comfortably, and making great memories together. That has a price, and it's far less than most people think.

Write your own answer down. Put a number on it if you can. Then look at where you are today.

## Give every euro a job

Guilt grows in the grey areas. When all your money sits in one big pot, every euro you spend feels like it's coming out of your future.

Split it instead:

- **Safety:** an emergency fund of a few months of essential costs, kept somewhere easy to reach.
- **Future:** money for long-term goals, like your pension or investments, set aside automatically every month.
- **Life now:** a planned amount every month that you're allowed to spend, guilt-free, on whatever you like.

Once safety and future are taken care of first, the "life now" money is no longer a risk. It's part of the plan.

## Practise spending, on purpose

This sounds strange, but spending is a skill too. If you've avoided it for years, start gently:

- **Pick one thing a month** from your "life now" money that you've been postponing.
- **Notice the guilt, then wait.** It usually fades within a day or two. Write down how you feel a week later.
- **Spend on experiences and people,** not only on things. A dinner with friends often brings more joy than another object.
- **Talk about it with your partner.** Agree on your "enough" number together, so the spending feels shared, not secret.

## When to get support

If the anxiety about money is affecting your sleep, your relationship or your daily life, you don't have to sort it out alone. A money coach can help you untangle the beliefs behind it. And if it feels heavier than that, speaking to a GP or a mental health professional is a sign of strength, not weakness.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why do I feel guilty spending money when I can afford it?

Often because of old money beliefs, learned in childhood or from a difficult experience, that still sound the alarm even though your situation has changed. The guilt is a feeling, not proof that you're doing something wrong.

### Is it bad to be too frugal?

Being careful is healthy. It becomes a problem when it stops you enjoying your life, causes tension with the people you love, or keeps you anxious no matter how much you save.

### How much should I spend on myself?

There's no single right number. Once your emergency fund and long-term saving are covered, many people find it helps to set a fixed amount or percentage of take-home pay to spend freely each month.

### How do I stop feeling anxious about money?

Start with clarity: know your numbers, decide what "enough" means, and give every euro a job. If the anxiety stays, talk it through with a coach or a professional.
