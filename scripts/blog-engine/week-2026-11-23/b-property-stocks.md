> **The short answer:** it's rarely "either, or". Property gives you rent you can see and touch, but it ties a lot of money to one asset in one country. Shares, usually through low-cost funds, spread your money widely and are easier to sell, but they move up and down more. Start from the life you want, then build a mix that gets you there and lets you sleep at night.

A couple wrote to me with a question I hear more than almost any other.

The writer is 39. Together with their wife, they had always invested in property, only in Malta. They own their home outright. They have two rented apartments on a loan, and the rent comfortably covers the repayments. By the time they're about 60, both will be paid off.

Now they have over €200,000 in cash. Their goal is to stop working, or at least slow down a lot, in about ten years.

Their friends have done well in the stock market. One told them she had averaged around 15% a year since 2012. But the writer has always kept away from it. Their parents lost a lot of money when they invested through one of Malta's biggest banks, and that memory never left.

Their question: should they buy a third apartment, or finally invest in stocks?

## Firstly, notice where the fear comes from

This hesitation isn't really about stocks. It's about what happened to their parents.

I understand this personally. My own father lost money in the 1990s on what was then seen as one of the most attractive investments around: Argentinian bonds. That experience shaped how I saw investing for years.

What it taught me is this: not every investment is a good investment, and not all "stock market investing" is the same. A bad product sold by a bank is not the same thing as a low-cost fund that holds thousands of companies.

And the friend's 15% a year? Maybe. But results like that depend on what she bought, when, and for how long. Comparing yourself to a friend's best number is a poor way to plan.

## What a third apartment would really cost

Let's put some simple numbers on it. These are an illustration, not a quote.

Say a €200,000 apartment rents for €900 a month:

- **Stamp duty:** in Malta it's normally 5% of the price, so about **€10,000**, before notary and agent fees.
- **Rent:** €900 × 12 = **€10,800** a year.
- **Tax:** if you choose Malta's 15% final tax on rent, it's charged on the gross rent, so about **€1,620**.
- **What's left:** about **€9,180** a year, before repairs, empty months and furniture.

That's a return of roughly 4.4% on the €210,000 you'd put in, before those other costs. Not bad. But it also means that nearly everything they own, their home and three apartments, would sit in one asset class, in one small country.

That's called concentration risk. When property does well, it feels like genius. When it doesn't, everything is hit at the same time.

## What does "retiring at 50" actually need?

Before choosing between bricks and shares, I always ask one question: what does your version of retirement look like?

Do you want to stop completely, or just work less? How much do you realistically need each month? Which costs will stay, and which will disappear once the loans are paid?

Early retirement isn't about chasing the highest return. It's about matching reliable income to your future costs. Rent is good at this. So are investments that you can draw on gradually. Knowing your number tells you how much of each you need.

## A gentler way in

If you've never invested before, putting €200,000 into the market in one day is a lot to handle emotionally. If prices drop 10% or 20% soon after, even a sensible long-term plan can feel like a disaster.

A smoother approach is to invest gradually. For example, investing €200,000 in equal parts over 24 months is about **€8,333 a month**. Some months you'll buy when prices are high, some when they're low. This is called dollar-cost averaging, and its biggest benefit is that it helps you stay calm and consistent.

And remember: adding shares doesn't mean giving up on property. It means not depending on a single path.

## Educate yourself first, then get advice

On a sum this size, speak to a licensed investment advisor before you decide. Not to hand over control, but to understand your options and how they fit your goals and tax situation.

But learn the basics first. It's your money, and no one will ever care about it more than you do. Knowing how a fund works, what it costs and what can go wrong is what turns fear into a decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is property or the stock market a better investment?

Neither wins every time. Over the past century, the S&P 500 has averaged around 8% to 10% a year, with some painful years. Property can earn rent and grow in value, but it costs more to buy and sell and keeps your money in one place. Many people hold some of both.

### Is buying property in Malta still a good investment?

It can be, but run the numbers first: stamp duty, tax on rent, repairs, empty months and the loan. Then ask how much of your wealth is already in Malta property.

### How much tax do I pay on rental income in Malta?

You can choose to pay a 15% final tax on the gross rent, with no expenses deducted. Otherwise rent is taxed with your other income. Check which suits you with a tax professional.

### Should I invest a large sum all at once?

Investing in parts over months is often easier to live with emotionally. Historically, investing all at once has done better about two thirds of the time, according to Vanguard research, because markets rise more often than they fall. The best choice is the one you'll stick with.
