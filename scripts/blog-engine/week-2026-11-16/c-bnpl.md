> **The short answer:** buy now, pay later can be a useful tool, but only if you could afford to pay in full today, you know the total cost before you sign, and you have one plan at a time. Used to buy things you can't afford, it quietly turns into expensive debt. And from 20 November 2026, new EU rules treat most of these plans as consumer credit.

A 28-year-old reader bought the latest iPhone on a buy now, pay later plan. Price: €2,000. Total she'll eventually pay: €2,565.

Her mum was furious. "Irresponsible," she said. "Reckless."

The daughter's answer surprised me. She had the €2,000. She chose not to spend it, so she'd keep a cash buffer. And the phone wasn't a luxury. It was the main tool for her growing side business as an influencer.

So who was right?

## The real cost, in plain numbers

Let's start with the part that matters most. That plan cost her **€565 extra**. That's about 28% on top of the price.

Put another way, she paid more than a quarter of a phone for the privilege of not paying for it today. Sometimes that's a fair trade. Often it isn't.

The trouble with buy now, pay later is that it's designed to feel free. Small monthly amounts. One tap at checkout. No forms. And that's exactly why it's easy to stack three or four plans without noticing.

## Firstly, when it can make sense

I'll say something that might surprise you: I understood her decision.

Spending every euro of your savings just to avoid a loan isn't automatically responsible. If your emergency fund is empty and the boiler breaks next month, you're in a worse position than someone with a small, planned repayment and cash in the bank.

Buy now, pay later can work when:

- **You could pay in full today,** and you're choosing not to for a good reason.
- **The purchase earns or saves you money,** like a tool for your work.
- **The plan is interest-free,** or you know the exact total you'll pay.
- **You pay it off early** once your cash flow allows, to cut the cost.

## And when it's a trap

It becomes a problem when:

- **You're using it because you can't afford it,** not because you're choosing to.
- **You have more than one plan running,** and you're not sure of the total.
- **It's for something you want, not something you need,** especially in a sale.
- **You've never checked the late fees.** Missed payments can get expensive fast.

Black Friday is the perfect storm for this. Big discounts, countdown timers and a "pay in 4" button right under the price. A 30% discount means nothing if you pay 28% more to spread it.

## What changes on 20 November

From 20 November 2026, the EU's new Consumer Credit Directive applies across all member states, Malta included. For the first time, it brings most buy now, pay later arrangements under consumer credit law, even interest-free ones with short repayment terms.

In practice, that means more protection and clearer information before you sign. It doesn't change the most important rule, though: the cheapest credit is still the credit you don't need.

## Four questions to ask before you click "pay later"

1. Could I pay for this in full today without touching my emergency fund?
2. What is the total I'll pay, including any fees or interest?
3. How many other plans am I paying right now?
4. Would I still buy this if I had to pay cash?

If you can't answer the first two in ten seconds, close the tab and sleep on it.

## The generational argument

Her mum's approach was simple: save first, buy later. She took her first real holiday at 40. It worked for her.

Today's world moves faster, and opportunities come and go quickly. But here's what both generations get right. The goal was never to avoid debt at all costs, or to use it freely. It's to stay in control. Debt you planned and chose is a tool. Debt that crept up on you is a weight.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is buy now, pay later a loan?

Yes, in effect. You receive the item now and repay over time. From 20 November 2026, most of these plans fall under EU consumer credit rules.

### Does buy now, pay later affect my credit?

It can. Missed or late payments can lead to fees and, depending on the provider, may affect your ability to borrow in future. Check the terms before you sign.

### Is it better to pay in full or use buy now, pay later?

If paying in full doesn't leave you without an emergency fund, paying in full is usually cheaper. If it would empty your savings, a small, interest-free plan you pay off early can be the safer choice.

### How do I get out of several buy now, pay later plans?

List every plan with its amount and end date. Stop adding new ones, pay the smallest off first for a quick win, and keep going until they're gone.
