> **The short answer:** nobody knows exactly which jobs AI will change, so don't plan from panic. Build an emergency fund that covers six to twelve months of essential expenses, keep investing (even if a little less), and invest in the skills AI is still poor at: judgment, relationships and understanding the business you work in.

A reader wrote to me recently. She's 38, a software engineer earning €75,000 a year, with a home loan, three children, and the bigger salary in the family.

Two months earlier, a colleague with seven years at the company was let go. Not for bad performance. The company had introduced AI tools that could do in hours what used to take his team days.

"I always felt my job was the most secure," she wrote. "Now I'm thinking of saving €100,000 and stopping my investments until I get there. Am I overthinking this?"

## Firstly, you're not overthinking it

For years we assumed automation would come for repetitive, low-paid work first. That's no longer the whole picture. Desk jobs built on producing text, code, reports or analysis are now squarely in the conversation, and many of them are well paid.

So if you work at a desk and this worries you, the worry is reasonable. Having said that, worry is a terrible financial planner. It picks big round numbers and makes them feel safe.

## Is €100,000 the right number?

Maybe. But let's find out instead of guessing.

Your emergency fund should be based on your **essential** monthly costs, not your lifestyle. Think only of what you can't avoid:

- Home loan or rent
- Groceries
- Utilities and bills
- Insurance
- Childcare and school costs
- Loan repayments

{{img:b-worksheet|Worksheet: list your essential monthly costs (home loan or rent, groceries, utilities, insurance, childcare, loan repayments, transport to work), add them up, then multiply by 6 and by 12.}}

Add them up. For someone who is the main earner, with children and a mortgage, six to twelve months of these essentials is a sensible buffer. If that truly comes to €100,000, then aim for it. Most people are surprised at how much lower it comes out once they strip it back to the needs.

And remember, your partner's income counts. If the household still has one salary coming in, the gap you need to cover is smaller.

## Don't stop investing completely

This is where I'd push back gently. Halting all investing to build a giant cash pile feels safe, but it has a cost. Every month you stop, you lose time in the market, and time is what does the heavy lifting.

A better balance:

- **Reduce your monthly investing for a while,** and send the difference to your emergency fund.
- **Keep your investing habit alive,** even at a lower amount.
- **Once your safety net is built,** go back to your normal investing amount.

I've coached many people through job loss. The ones who came through it with the least damage weren't always the ones with the biggest savings. They were the ones whose money had been working quietly in the background for years.

## The part that isn't about money

AI is very good at executing tasks. It's still weak at judgment, at reading a client, at understanding context, at knowing when the technically correct answer is the wrong business decision.

So ask yourself honestly:

- What do I bring beyond the task itself?
- Do I understand the business I work in, not just my part of it?
- Am I the person others come to when things are unclear?
- How visible is that in my day-to-day work?

The people least exposed won't be the ones who ignore AI. They'll be the ones who use it well, and bring the human part it can't.

## Three things to do this month

1. **Work out your essential monthly costs** and multiply by six. That's your first target.
2. **Set up an automatic transfer** to a separate, easy-access savings account the day after payday.
3. **Learn one AI tool properly** for your own job. Being the person who uses it best is a strong position.

You're not overreacting. But you're also in a stronger position than you think. Use this moment of clarity well.

## Frequently asked questions

### How big should my emergency fund be if I'm worried about my job?

Six to twelve months of essential expenses is a common range for main earners with dependants. Base it on your needs, not your current lifestyle.

### Should I stop investing to save more cash?

Usually not completely. Reducing your contributions for a while, while keeping the habit going, protects both your safety net and your long-term growth.

### Where should I keep my emergency fund?

Somewhere safe and easy to reach quickly, like a separate savings account. Avoid investing it, because you may need it at a time when markets are down.

### Which jobs are safest from AI?

No one can say for certain. Roles that rely on judgment, relationships, hands-on work and understanding people tend to be harder to automate than tasks done alone at a screen.
